# Position Extrapolation and Velocity Feedforward for UAV Landing on Moving Platforms with Delayed Observations

English content review draft 15 October 2026

## Abstract

An unmanned aerial vehicle returning to a moving platform must track, align, and descend using position observations that are already old. Reducing tracking error alone does not ensure that descent conditions are met. This study compares sample-and-hold, current-position extrapolation, extrapolation with velocity feedforward, and hold with velocity feedforward within a common proportional–integral–derivative controller and landing state machine. Paired simulations separate the effects of the two modules and examine sensitivity to integral gain, initial offset, and observation faults. In the nominal variable-speed case, 30 paired tracking runs per configuration reduced mean horizontal root-mean-square error from 0.227 m with hold to 0.0430 m with extrapolation and feedforward. Additional tests showed that changing integral gain enabled previously unsuccessful configurations to descend, while reducing the initial offset reversed the low-speed error ranking. All three fault-test configurations completed after a short observation outage, whereas all timed out under a persistent 600 ms delay. Separate ground-truth dynamics simulations checked flight-controller integration and task execution. The results support joint evaluation of compensation, feedback settings, and descent criteria within the tested conditions.

Keywords: UAV; moving-platform landing; observation delay; position extrapolation; velocity feedforward

## 1 Introduction

An unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) returning to a vehicle in motion must align with a landing point that continues to move throughout approach and descent. Position observations may have aged during acquisition, processing, or transmission. Holding the latest received position can therefore make the UAV track a point the platform has already passed. The controller must both use old observations at the current instant and supply the velocity needed to follow the platform. Descent criteria can then turn a tracking offset into a task-level constraint.

Moving-platform recovery studies cover complete takeoff, tracking, and landing procedures, high-speed ground vehicles, and low-cost systems [1,2,3,4]. Relative-motion sensing approaches include image-based visual servoing [5,6], camera arrays [7], and visual-inertial fusion [8]; a review summarizes perception and control requirements [9]. Control approaches include feedback with prediction [10], model predictive control [11,12], learning methods [13,14], and vector-field guidance [15]. Recent visual guidance also addresses target visibility and inner-loop response assumptions [16]. Errors reported across different sensing interfaces, control inputs, and environments are not directly comparable.

Delay in coordinated landing [17], visual perception delay [18], and current-state estimation from delayed sampled outputs [19] have already been studied. Velocity feedforward (FF) has been used in shipboard visual servoing [20] and mechanically assisted recovery [21]. Here, timestamped world-frame positions form the input interface. Extrapolation compensates displacement between acquisition and control, while feedforward adds estimated platform velocity to the command. The study evaluates established compensation ideas rather than proposing a new prediction theory.

Sample-and-hold (S&H) serves as an ablation reference with platform extrapolation disabled; it is not presented as a reproduced default of a particular open-source autopilot. Four configurations complete the two-module combination. They share controller settings, with a separate integral-gain sensitivity check. This does not establish performance after exhaustive tuning of each method.

Approach motion, observation loss, and contact conditions also affect completion [22,23]. The study quantifies module effects under a common controller, examines their interaction with descent admission through speed, gain, and initial-offset tests, and compares waiting, recovery, and timeout behavior under matched faults. Separate dynamics tasks check implementation through a flight controller.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Observations and configurations

UAV and platform positions use an east-north-up world frame and retain acquisition timestamps. The control instant is t_k, the latest accepted acquisition time is t_s, and observation age is Δ=t_k−t_s≥0. Simulation clocks are shared. Duplicate, out-of-order, non-finite, and incorrectly framed samples are rejected. Different sampling rates do not imply compensation for unknown clock offsets.

True platform velocity is used only for evaluation and ideal attachment. Feedforward uses velocity estimated from position history. These are retained settings rather than optimized values derived from an error budget.

### 2.2 From motion to current-position extrapolation

Integrating velocity between acquisition and control gives

$$
\mathbf p_g(t_k)=\mathbf p_g(t_s)+\int_{t_s}^{t_k}\mathbf v_g(\tau)\,d\tau\tag{1}
$$

Assuming nearly constant velocity over a short interval, the latest position sample y_g,s and estimated velocity v̂_g define

$$
\hat{\mathbf p}_g(t_k)=\mathbf y_{g,s}+\hat{\mathbf v}_g\Delta\tag{2}
$$

Velocity is obtained by fitting a line with an intercept to samples H in a 0.40 s window ending at the latest acquisition time. Let t̄ and ȳ denote mean acquisition time and position, and q_i=t_i−t̄. Minimizing squared position residuals yields

$$
\tilde{\mathbf v}_g=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}q_i(\mathbf y_{g,i}-\bar{\mathbf y})}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}q_i^2}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}q_i\mathbf y_{g,i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}q_i^2}\tag{3}
$$

The unconstrained estimate ṽ_g is limited to a speed norm of 3.0 m/s to obtain v̂_g. If the window contains fewer than two samples, the last two available samples are used. Fewer than two historical samples, or a time-deviation sum below 10⁻¹² s², returns zero velocity. Position extrapolation remains anchored to the latest sample rather than to a fitted position. The least-squares derivation, fallback conditions, and error decomposition are retained in Supplement S6.

Extrapolation error includes position error, historical velocity-estimation error, and uncompensated acceleration. Given bounds on these terms, a conditional bound contains constant, linear, and quadratic terms in age. Global position and velocity-error bounds were not calibrated, so this relation is not used as a closed-loop accuracy or stability guarantee.

### 2.3 Feedforward and implemented commands

Let e(t) denote true horizontal platform position minus true horizontal UAV position. Its derivative equals platform velocity minus UAV velocity. With proportional–integral–derivative (PID) feedback f_PID based on estimated position error, the unsaturated command is

$$
\mathbf r_{xy}(t)=b_{ff}\hat{\mathbf v}_{g,xy}(t)+\mathbf f_{PID}(t)\tag{4}
$$

Only when velocity tracking is approximately ideal and the command is unsaturated does substitution into the relative-motion relation give

$$
\dot{\mathbf e}(t)\approx\mathbf v_{g,xy}(t)-b_{ff}\hat{\mathbf v}_{g,xy}(t)-\mathbf f_{PID}(t)\tag{5}
$$

Accurate feedforward supplies part of the translation velocity; feedback still corrects relative-position error. Integral feedback can also supply constant following velocity without feedforward. Equation (5) therefore does not imply improvement in every condition.

For each horizontal axis j, the implementation computes

$$
w_{j,k}=\operatorname{clip}(b_{ff}\hat v_{g,j,k}+K_p\hat e_{j,k}+K_iI_{j,k}+K_dD_{j,k},V_{xy})\tag{6}
$$

Here ê_j,k is estimated position error, I_j,k is integral state, and D_j,k is filtered error rate. The switch b_ff is zero for A/B and one for C/D. Integral state is limited to 3 m·s. Conditional integration accepts or rejects a candidate update using the direction of per-axis saturation. The derivative-filter time constant is 0.10 s. After axis limits, the vector w_k is limited again:

$$
\mathbf v^{cmd}_{xy,k}=\begin{cases}\mathbf w_k,&\|\mathbf w_k\|\le V_{xy},\\ V_{xy}\mathbf w_k/\|\mathbf w_k\|,&\|\mathbf w_k\|>V_{xy}.\end{cases}\tag{7}
$$

The vector limit does not feed back into the integrator. Nominal gains are Kp=1.2 s⁻¹, Ki=0.15 s⁻², and Kd=0.08, with V_xy=2.0 m/s. Sensitivity tests change only Ki to 0.30; no optimized winner is selected. Full discrete updates are retained in S6.

### 2.4 Descent and invalid observations

TRACK maintains a height of 2.0 m above the platform. DESCEND requires estimated horizontal error at most 0.18 m, horizontal relative speed at most 0.15 m/s, and height error at most 0.15 m for 1.5 s. The height reference then decreases at 0.25 m/s to no lower than 0.04 m below deck level. Vertical feedback gain is 1.5 s⁻¹ with a 0.6 m/s limit. Horizontal error above 0.35 m or relative speed above 0.30 m/s returns the controller to TRACK.

Invalid observations trigger HOLD, reset PID and height references, and command zero world-frame velocity. This is neither instantaneous stopping nor relative hovering. Valid observations must be followed by renewed tracking and alignment. Contact, horizontal alignment, relative height between −0.03 and 0.06 m, and vertical relative speed at most 0.12 m/s must persist for 0.50 s before LANDED.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Model and study matrix

The point-mass model uses a 0.02 s step, a 0.25 s first-order velocity time constant, and a 2.0 m/s² acceleration limit per axis. Deck height is 0.50 m. The nominal UAV starts at (−2,1,2.5) m with zero velocity. Platform trajectories are

$$
x_g(t)=\begin{cases}0,&\mathrm{stationary},\\0.5t,&\mathrm{constant},\\0.5t+0.625[1-\cos(0.4t)],&\mathrm{variable}.\end{cases}\tag{8}
$$

Variable platform speed ranges from 0.25 to 0.75 m/s. Contact requires reaching deck height within a horizontal radius of 0.30 m. Ideal attachment then overwrites velocity. Precontact relative speed is recorded before this overwrite; impact, bounce, and tipping are not modeled.

Reused logs in Table 2 are not new trials. The original 2414 runs plus 780 additions give 3194 unique executions; heterogeneous conditions are not pooled into one success rate. The supplementary protocol was frozen before execution. Source snapshots, logs, summaries, and hashes are retained. All 780 new logs were independently recomputed for error, contact, and completion; platform observations were matched on common time prefixes across 990 new or reused records.

Nominal transport has 180 ms delay, uniform ±30 ms jitter, 2% independent loss, and no position noise. Platform and UAV nominal sampling rates are 50 and 30 Hz; events occur on a 20 ms grid, giving a mean received age of about 190 ms. Fault tests separately stop platform-sample generation during 12–14 s, add independent Gaussian position noise with 0.02 m standard deviation per axis, or impose a fixed 600 ms delay without jitter. These are prescribed interface disturbances, not a calibrated camera model. Wind, actual vision, and unknown clock offsets are absent.

### 3.2 Metrics and paired statistics

Tracking root-mean-square error (RMSE) uses 2501 samples during 10–60 s:

$$
\mathrm{RMSE}_{xy}=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N\|\mathbf p_{u,xy,k}-\mathbf p_{g,xy,k}\|^2}\tag{9}
$$

A whole run is the statistical unit; communication seeds are paired within each condition. Landing completes only after contact and LANDED by 60 s, with every scheduled attempt retained in the denominator. Contact error, precontact relative speed, and completion time are conditional on completion; failures are not filled with zeros. Means have sample standard deviations and individual 95% Student t intervals. Completion uses exact Clopper–Pearson intervals [24,25], without a simultaneous-coverage claim. The primary window is unchanged; shorter-window analyses are exploratory.

### 3.3 Dynamics task execution

Gazebo Harmonic 8.15.0 and ArduCopter SITL receive ENU-to-NED velocity commands through MAVLink. This layer runs C only with ground-truth positions and no injected observation delay. It uses 2.5 m tracking height, 1.5 m/s horizontal limit, 0.18 m/s descent, alignment thresholds of 0.20 m and 0.22 m/s for 2 s, and additional attitude and heading checks. A wheel-driven platform travels at about 0.35 m/s with a 2×1.4 m deck. Five stationary and five moving nominal tasks, plus one task for each of five additional starts under each motion condition, give 20 heterogeneous executions.

Completion checks deck contact, LANDED, COMPLETE, and disarming. Reference-point curves use the latest object poses without cross-object time interpolation and are not ranked against point-mass contact accuracy. This layer checks integration, not between-configuration compensation performance under delay.

## 4 Results and Discussion

### 4.1 Effects of the two modules

The four configurations in Figure 2 are compared over 30 matched seeds. A/B/C reuse the same subset of the original 100-seed study; D is new. Variable-speed mean tracking RMSE for A/B/C/D is 0.227, 0.153, 0.043, 0.0973 m, and first-contact error is 0.299, 0.186, 0.032, 0.0667 m. D shows a benefit from feedforward even with platform extrapolation disabled. C further reduces these errors, although it is not best in every metric. Full paired intervals and completion times are in S1.

### 4.2 Low speed and the approach transient

At 0.25 m/s in the original study, B/C tracking RMSE is 0.0127/0.0194 m and contact error is 0.0286/0.0438 m. This condition has no injected position noise. Across 80 associated logs, C's maximum horizontal platform-velocity estimation error after 1 s during valid tracking or descent is about 9.42×10⁻¹² m/s. Platform-velocity noise dominance therefore does not explain this observation.

New seeds 142–156 reproduce the nominal-start ranking. Changing only the initial position to (−0.5,0.25,2.5) m gives B/C tracking RMSE of 0.0118 / 0.00748 m and contact error of 0.0312 / 0.0198 m, reversing the ranking. Figure 3 shows decaying error and integral velocity contributions. B gradually supplies following velocity through integration; C supplies most platform translation through feedforward. Initial conditions affect the finite-window comparison, but the test also changes the approach history and controller state. It does not isolate integral, saturation, and other transient contributions individually.

### 4.3 Integral limits and descent admission

At 0.75 m/s, original A/B runs did not descend. Nominal integral velocity is limited to Ki×3=0.45 m/s. In an approximately steady, unsaturated condition, proportional feedback supplies the remainder, requiring about (0.75−0.45)/1.2=0.25 m estimated error, above the 0.18 m admission threshold. This is a joint parameter-and-threshold limitation, not a universal vehicle speed limit.

For new seeds, Ki=0.15 gives A/B/C completions of 0/15, 0/15, and 15/15. With Ki=0.30, all three complete 15/15. B/C high-speed tracking RMSE then becomes 0.00553 / 0.0069 m and contact error 0.00458 / 0.00558 m. Feedback-only B can complete after a parameter change, so the original failure does not establish that feedforward is necessary. At low speed, tracking and contact rankings under the larger gain are also not identical; see S2.

### 4.5 Engineering implications and scope

Compensation reduces feedback burden under specific motion and transport conditions, but its use depends on integral capability, approach state, and descent admission. The four-combination check supports nominal benefits; gain and initial-condition tests show that rankings and completion can change. Tracking, contact conditions, and completion therefore require separate assessment rather than ranking solely by percentage improvement over A.

Finite-history fitting and PID commands require neither online trajectory optimization nor model training, but target-processor latency, memory, and energy have not been measured. Ideal attachment, single-axis motion, and prescribed transport limit generalization. Ground-truth dynamics tasks do not validate vision, coordinate recovery, unknown clock offsets, wind, or physical contact. Full derivations and reproducible records define the implementation and evidence scope; they do not establish whole-system stability or flight safety.

## 5 Conclusion

Four module combinations quantify the nominal effects of current-position extrapolation and estimated velocity feedforward within a common controller and descent logic. Additional controls refine the interpretation: integral-gain changes enable previously unsuccessful configurations to descend, initial-offset changes reverse the low-speed error ranking, and persistent excess delay causes all three tested configurations to time out. Compensation must therefore be assessed jointly with feedback settings, approach history, and observation validity. Ground-truth dynamics tasks provide a separate integration check; actual vision and flight remain unvalidated.
